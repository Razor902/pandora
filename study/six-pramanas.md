## The idea in one line

In Hindu philosophy, especially Advaita Vedanta and the Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā school, valid knowledge (jñāna) comes through six independent means, called pramāṇas, instruments or ways of right knowing. Other schools accept fewer: Nyāya accepts four, Sāṃkhya three. The full set of six is characteristic of Advaita.

## The six

1. **Pratyakṣa (Perception)** — Direct knowledge through the five senses, or the mind as a sixth sense in some accounts. The foundation of the others, most reliable in the empirical realm. Example: "I see a tree."

2. **Anumāna (Inference)** — Knowledge from logical reasoning based on invariable concomitance (vyāpti). Example: "There is fire on the hill because there is smoke," smoke being invariably associated with fire.

3. **Upamāna (Comparison / Analogy)** — Knowledge gained by recognizing similarity. Example: knowing a wild animal is a gavaya (wild cow) because it resembles a domestic cow after being told so.

4. **Śabda (Verbal Testimony / Scriptural Authority)** — Knowledge from reliable verbal sources, especially the Vedas and Upanishads (considered apauruṣeya, not of human origin) or trustworthy experts. Crucial for knowledge of Brahman, Ātman, karma, and rebirth, which cannot be known by the senses or pure logic alone.

5. **Arthāpatti (Presumption / Postulation)** — Knowledge arrived at by assuming something to explain an otherwise inexplicable fact. Example: "Devadatta is fat but never eats during the day, so he must eat at night."

6. **Anupalabdhi (Non-apprehension / Non-perception)** — Knowledge of the absence of something through not perceiving it under conditions where it should have been perceived if present. Example: "There is no pot on the floor," because you do not see one and the conditions for seeing it are met.

## How they work together

Perception and inference handle the empirical world. Verbal testimony, especially scripture, is essential for transcendental knowledge. The others fill logical and experiential gaps. In Advaita practice, they support the path of śravaṇa (hearing the teaching), manana (reflection), and nididhyāsana (deep meditation), leading to direct realization of the Self (aparokṣa anubhūti).

## Related but distinct

- **Six Darśanas** (philosophical schools): Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, and Vedānta. These are systems of thought, not layers of knowledge itself.
- **Vedāṅgas**: six auxiliary disciplines for studying the Vedas (phonetics, grammar, and others).
- **Parā vs. aparā vidyā** (higher vs. lower knowledge, from the Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad): a broader distinction, rather than six layers.
